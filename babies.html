A baby changes the whole family.

It's tempting to think babies are unaware of their parents' conflict. They notice far more than we assume. They may not understand our words yet, but they are taking in the tone, the tension, and the rhythm of the household around them.

And whether this is your first baby or your fifth, a baby widens the small cracks that were already there. Exhausted, overwhelmed parents rarely get the chance to step back and decide, together, who does what and when.

I work with families of children three and under, whether you're expecting, staying together, separating, or not sure yet.

If you're working through this together

You don't have to have decided anything about your relationship to talk with me. This isn't couples therapy. It's a practical conversation about the household you're living in right now: who handles nights, how the work gets divided, and what happens when you disagree in front of the baby.

Some couples leave with a family agreement, a written plan for the roles, routines, and expectations that let them stay together more happily. Others realize they need something different. Either way, you only have to commit to one conversation to start. Nothing moves forward until everyone understands what a further conversation would be designed to do.

If you're separating

Babies and toddlers need a different kind of parenting plan. They live in the present, a week feels very long to them, and they build bonds through frequent, predictable time with each parent. A schedule designed for a school-aged child can work against a baby.

With a background in infant-toddler development and a PITC Fellowship, I help parents build plans that fit how very young children attach, sleep, and handle transitions:

  • Frequent contact with both parents, sized to your child's age
  • Feeding, naps, and bedtime routines that stay consistent across two homes
  • Step-up schedules that grow as your child does, with built-in review dates
  • Exchange Recipes: handoffs between homes built around your child's temperament
  • A simple way to share information about your child between homes

I work with families virtually across California, and I'm glad to work alongside your attorneys, therapists, or other professionals.

Why Laura?

I came to this work through the classroom. When my daughter started preschool, I was transfixed, and I went on to work with infants and toddlers and, eventually, to supervise the teachers caring for them. Babies who can't yet tell you what they need are a puzzle to be solved: what does this small person need to reach the next milestone? The longer I worked on that puzzle, the clearer it became that the answer usually ran through the family.

So much of that work happened at drop-off and pickup, with new parents who were exhausted, anxious, and often disagreeing about what their baby needed. My fellowship through the Program for Infant-Toddler Care (PITC) ended with a unit on conflict within families and across cultures, and that's what led me back to mediation. It taught me that disagreements about sleep, feeding, and comfort are rarely just about the baby. They carry each parent's upbringing, culture, and deeply held beliefs about what good care looks like.

That fellowship also gave shape to the rule I still work by: seek to understand the parents, don't judge them. It isn't always easy to hold, which is exactly why it matters.

Temperament charting and Exchange Recipes

When my daughter was young and I was studying infant-toddler mental health, I discovered a tool called temperament charting. It helped me see that my daughter and I clashed so often because we were both highly persistent, with strong emotions. Once I understood that, I learned to plan ahead for the hard moments, and to step out and let my husband step in when we got stuck. It was a game changer for our family, and now I use the same tool to help other families.

A preschool teacher is nothing if not a master of transitions: outside to inside, play to nap, eating to handwashing. Teachers use temperament charting to observe a child and understand the best way to help them through those transitions. In the same way, we work together to craft what I call Exchange Recipes: plans for exchanges between homes, built around your children's temperamental needs, to bring more peace to your family.

Frequently asked questions

When a new baby strains your relationship
Is it normal to resent my partner after having a baby?

Yes. Resentment after a baby is one of the most common and least talked-about parts of new parenthood. It usually doesn't mean your relationship is failing. More often, it means roles, time, and the day-to-day work of caring for a baby need to be talked about on purpose.

What is the "mental load"?

It's the invisible work of running a family: remembering when the baby last ate, noticing you're low on diapers, booking the pediatrician, planning ahead. When one parent carries most of it, that parent often ends up feeling alone, and the other often feels shut out. Sharing it means handing over whole jobs, not just tasks.

Can mediation help if we're not getting divorced?

Yes. Some couples come to me to sort out roles, schedules, and expectations so they can stay together more happily. We write a family agreement instead of a divorce agreement. Others do decide to separate, and mediation helps them build a parenting plan that fits their baby.

When should we talk to a therapist or doctor about postpartum depression?

Mediation helps you think through what help looks like, talk about it with each other, and build a plan. But if you need more than a clear plan, you may want to talk with a therapist or doctor in addition to this work. That's true for either parent. If sadness, worry, or numbness doesn't lift, feels heavy most days, or gets in the way of caring for yourself or your baby, please reach out.

Having help on the outside can also make your plan in mediation more realistic and more helpful. The two work best together.

You can call or text the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262). It's free, confidential, and available 24/7 in English, Spanish, and many other languages. Postpartum Support International's HelpLine at 1-800-944-4773 can connect you with support and local resources. It serves mothers, fathers, and family members. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911. For a mental health crisis, call or text 988.

Can grandparents or other caregivers be part of mediation?

Yes. When a grandparent, nanny, or other family member is a big part of your baby's care, it can help to include them. Everyone gets a clear role, and parenting decisions stay with the parents. Grandparents-to-be may also like my class, Preparing to Be Grandparents.

Parenting plans for babies and toddlers
Why do babies and toddlers need frequent time with both parents?

Babies and toddlers live in the present, and a week feels very long to them. Research suggests babies form meaningful bonds with both parents at about the same time, around 6 to 7 months, even when one parent does more of the care. Bonds need regular contact to grow. Frequent doesn't have to mean hours every day. An hour or two, several times a week, can make a real difference.

Is every other weekend okay for a baby?

For most babies, it's a hard fit. Two weeks is a very long time in a baby's life, and long gaps make it harder for a bond to grow. California's public policy is that children should have frequent and continuing contact with both parents after separation, unless that contact wouldn't be in the child's best interests.

Can my baby have overnights with the other parent?

It depends on your baby, and researchers still disagree. Some argue that frequent overnights with both parents do more good than harm in most cases. Others have found a link between frequent overnights and less secure attachment in infants, though the link was less clear for toddlers. Many families start with frequent daytime time and add overnights as their baby grows. A plan with a review date lets you adjust as you go.

How does breastfeeding work with a parenting plan?

Short daytime visits, often timed between feedings, let a breastfed baby spend time with both parents. If the nursing parent pumps, the other parent can give a bottle, which is also a wonderful bonding moment. A good plan spells out how feeding information is shared and changes as your baby starts solids or weans. A lactation consultant can be a helpful part of your support team.

What is a step-up parenting plan?

It's a schedule that gradually increases time as your child develops and adapts. It often starts with short, frequent visits and grows toward longer time and overnights.

What is the "witching hour," and how can a parenting plan help?

It's the late-afternoon stretch when babies and toddlers are overtired and dinner and bedtime feel impossible. A plan can bring the other parent in during that time to make dinner, give the bath, and help with bedtime. Your child gets steady time with a parent they love, and the parent who's been carrying the load gets real help.

What if I don't trust the other parent with our baby yet?

Sometimes that feeling is about confidence, and sometimes it's about safety. Both deserve to be taken seriously. If you have any safety concerns, tell me privately before we meet together. The process looks different, and some situations need other support first. If it's about confidence, a plan with short visits, hands-on routines, and a simple shared log can help trust grow week by week.

What if the other parent keeps controlling how I care for our baby?

This is often called gatekeeping, and it usually comes from love and worry, not bad intentions. It can happen with any parent who has done most of the early care. A clear plan with defined routines, a simple way to share information, and agreed limits on check-ins can help both parents relax into their roles.

If the control goes beyond the baby's care, to money, friends, where you go, or who you talk to, that's something different, and I'd want to talk with you privately first.

About mediation with me
Why work with a mediator who was an infant-toddler teacher?

Before returning to mediation, I spent years in infant-toddler classrooms. I hold Master Infant-Toddler Teacher credentials and I'm a PITC Fellow. I know what babies and toddlers need at each stage, and I've watched many families through the drop-off door. That lets me help you build a plan that fits your child's age, not just your calendar.

How is mediation different from couples therapy?

Therapy often helps you understand feelings and patterns, including where they came from. Mediation looks forward: what do we each need, and what will we actually do? Many families use both. I often work alongside therapists, and I'm glad to.

Is what we say in mediation private?

If your family goes to court, what you say can become part of the public court record. Mediation is different. In California, what you say in mediation is protected under California Evidence Code §1119 and generally can't be used later in court, with limited exceptions I'll explain before we begin. That lets you speak honestly about what's hard.

Do we need to go to court to make a parenting plan in California?

Many California parents build their plan together in mediation. To be enforceable, a parenting plan must be in writing, signed by both parents, and approved by a judge. A family law attorney can walk you through the court steps.

Can we do mediation online?

Yes. I work with families virtually all across California, so you can join from home, even during nap time.
